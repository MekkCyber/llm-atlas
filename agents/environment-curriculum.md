# Off-policy environment evolution (curriculum)
*Depth — one specific technique, grounded in its source paper(s).*

**TL;DR:** Once a frontier policy is strong, from-scratch synthesized environments stop teaching it anything. Prior co-evolution recipes re-synthesize environments based on the current policy's on-policy failures — but that couples environment design to the policy, limiting generalization. **Environment Evolution** (Fan et al., 2026) evolves environments **off-policy** along three difficulty-relevant axes derived from the multi-turn RL objective, and schedules generations of environments across training so the difficulty curve stays ahead of the policy.

## What it is

Three environment-design regimes in agent RL:

- **Static synthesis.** Generate a fixed pool of environments once; train against them. Difficulty saturates fast.
- **On-policy co-evolution.** Iterate: run rollouts, find where the policy fails, synthesize harder environments near those failure modes. Effective early, but the environments overfit to the *current* policy's blind spots and don't generalize.
- **Off-policy evolution.** Synthesize environments along axes that are known to raise difficulty, without conditioning on the training policy's rollouts. Environment Evolution is this regime.

## How it works

### Three evolution directions from the RL objective

Fan et al. derive three orthogonal axes that raise environment difficulty from the multi-turn learning objective:

1. **Task depth** — number of subgoals, tool-call chain length, or conditional branches required to solve.
2. **Environment breadth** — number of independent components (files, services, workspaces) the agent has to reason across.
3. **Feedback ambiguity** — how directly the environment's response indicates progress vs. requires interpretation.

Each axis is manipulated by a targeted synthesis operator (add a subgoal, add a dependency, obfuscate a signal).

### Loop-engineered multi-agent harness

Environments are generated by a multi-agent pipeline: one agent proposes an evolution direction, others implement the transformation, a critic agent verifies runnability. Because none of these agents is the *training* policy, the evolution is off-policy — the difficulty curve doesn't reflect the training policy's failure modes.

### Generation-by-generation scheduling

Environments are grouped into *generations*, and successive generations are used during successive training phases. The scheduler advances only when the training policy's success on the current generation crosses a threshold, so the training policy always faces environments just ahead of its capability.

## Why it matters

- **Continuous learning signal.** Off-policy evolution avoids the "environments become too easy once the policy plateaus" failure of static pools without inheriting the "environments overfit to this policy" failure of co-evolution.
- **Verifiable long-horizon RL that actually improves strong bases.** Qwen3.6-27B and Qwen3.6-35B-A3B trained on evolved environments gain **+14.4** and **+18.0 pts** on Terminal-Bench 2.1 respectively.
- **A recipe for environment scaling.** Frontier evolution drivers (Hy4 preview, Claude Opus 5, GPT-5.6 Sol) demonstrably produce harder environments than the training policy alone would; that reframes environment budget as *drivable by whichever model is currently strongest*, not by the training policy itself.

## Gotchas & tricks

- **Verify each evolved environment runs.** An "evolved" environment that doesn't build teaches the training policy tool-call patterns for tools that never resolve — actively harmful.
- **Difficulty-axis independence is aspirational.** Adding subgoals often adds breadth (new files) even when you didn't intend to; log the axis distributions across generations.
- **Off-policy ≠ blind to the policy.** You still need to *schedule* generations at the training policy's capability boundary; skipping to a generation five levels above collapses signal.
- **Combine with environment synthesis (Terminal-Universe) upstream.** Environment Evolution assumes a base of runnable environments; recovering them from trajectories is the complementary trick.
