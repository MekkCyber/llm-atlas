## 1. PerceptionDLM

**Authors:** Yueyi Sun, Yuhao Wang, Jason Li, Ye Tian, Tao Zhang, Jacky Mai, Yihan Wang, Haochen Wang, Jinbin Bai, Ling Yang, Yunhai Tong — *Peking University / ByteDance / WHU / NUS / CASIA*
**Links:** [arXiv:2606.19534](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.19534) · [HF](https://huggingface.co/papers/2606.19534) · [AlphaXiv](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2606.19534)
**Topics:** `multimodal`, `diffusion`, `efficient-inference`

### TL;DR

A multimodal diffusion language model (DLM) built around parallel region perception: instead of autoregressively captioning one region at a time, PerceptionDLM uses structured attention masks so a single denoising pass produces descriptions for many image regions simultaneously. A new "PerceptionDLM-Base" backbone gives state-of-the-art numbers among open-source diffusion MLLMs before the parallel-perception layer is added on top.

### Key idea

Treat region captioning as a *parallel decoding* problem. Each masked region gets its own prompt slot in the input sequence, and a hand-designed attention mask isolates each region's prompt from its peers while letting all of them attend to the shared visual tokens. Then a single denoising trajectory fills every region's caption at once — sequence-level *and* token-level parallelism, both natural for a DLM but not for an AR MLLM.

### Main finding

PerceptionDLM-Base sets a new SOTA among open-source diffusion MLLMs on standard visual-understanding benchmarks (exact numbers in paper). Layered on top, parallel region perception delivers the per-region throughput speedups you'd expect from issuing N captions in one pass instead of N sequential passes, at parity quality.

### Why it matters

Diffusion LMs have been pitched as "AR with parallel decoding" for two years, but most wins have been on toy text. PerceptionDLM is one of the cleanest cases where the parallelism actually matches a downstream workload (dense region captioning) — a hint at where DLMs will start to peel off real applications from AR MLLMs.

## 2. Reflective Masking

**Authors:** Yanming Zhang, Yihan Bian, Jingyuan Qi, Yuguang Yao, Lifu Huang, Tianyi Zhou — *University of Maryland / Virginia Tech / Intuit / UC Davis / MBZUAI*
**Links:** [arXiv:2606.16700](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.16700) · [HF](https://huggingface.co/papers/2606.16700) · [AlphaXiv](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2606.16700)
**Topics:** `diffusion`, `reasoning`, `post-training`

### TL;DR

Reflective Masking (RM) is a lightweight post-training recipe that teaches mask diffusion models (MDMs) to *revisit and locally edit* their own outputs over multiple denoising rounds — the MDM equivalent of CoT-style reflection in autoregressive models. No architecture changes; works as a native test-time-scaling knob across text, Sudoku, and image editing.

### Key idea

Use the MDM's masking operator as a *reasoning primitive*. After a first denoising pass produces a draft, RM re-masks a subset of tokens (the ones the model is least confident about, or that violate constraints) and denoises again, conditioned on the previous draft. A parameter-free "History Reference" mechanism reuses intermediate denoising states across turns so the model has memory of its earlier guesses — analogous to keeping the AR scratchpad around.

### Main finding

Across text generation, Sudoku, and image editing, multi-turn Reflective Masking beats standard single-pass masking baselines and a flat increase in denoising steps. The wins are biggest on tasks where the right answer needs *local* fixes (a few wrong tokens), exactly the regime where AR refinement is wasteful (regenerate the whole sequence) but RM only re-touches the broken spans.

### Why it matters

MDMs have struggled to compete with AR LMs on reasoning, because AR's "produce CoT, then condition on it" loop has no clean MDM analogue. RM is a credible answer: it gives MDMs a *native* test-time-scaling story that exploits exactly the property (local re-editing) AR loses by being sequential. If RM holds up at scale, it's the unlock for MDM reasoning.

## 3. MemSlides

**Authors:** Ye Jin, Yangyang Xu, Jun Zhu, Yibo Yang — *Tsinghua / BUPT / SJTU*
**Links:** [arXiv:2606.17162](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.17162) · [HF](https://huggingface.co/papers/2606.17162) · [AlphaXiv](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2606.17162)
**Topics:** `agents`, `memory`

### TL;DR

A hierarchical-memory agent framework for personalized slide generation. Memory is split into long-term (user profile + reusable tool experience) and working memory (preferences and constraints active during a multi-turn revision session). Combined with "scoped slide-local revision" so multi-turn edits only touch the affected region rather than regenerating the whole deck.

### Key idea

Three explicit memory stores with different write/read policies: **user-profile memory** (intent-conditioned, persists across sessions, drives round-0 personalization), **working memory** (carries the current session's new preferences and constraints), and **tool memory** (reusable execution experience for reliable localized edits). Plus a scoped-revision protocol that locates the smallest affected unit (a single slide region) before applying changes.

### Main finding

In controlled experiments: user-profile memory improves persona-alignment judgments on a multi-persona / multi-intent benchmark; tool-memory injection improves closed-loop modify behavior in matched-pair diagnostics; working memory carries preferences across rounds. The system's headline is a working separation of persistent vs. session vs. execution memory — each component is shown to do its job independently.

### Why it matters

Most "agent memory" pieces today are a single retrieval-augmented blob. MemSlides is one of the cleaner public arguments that the memory architecture should mirror the *temporal scope* of the information (lifetime / session / per-action), and that mixing them is what breaks personalization in multi-turn settings.

## 4. GateMem

**Authors:** Zhe Ren, Yibo Yang, Yimeng Chen, Zijun Zhao, Benshuo Fu, Zhihao Shu, Bingjie Zhang, Yangyang Xu, Dandan Guo, Shuicheng Yan — *(affiliations not listed on HF page)*
**Links:** [arXiv:2606.18829](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.18829) · [HF](https://huggingface.co/papers/2606.18829) · [AlphaXiv](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2606.18829)
**Topics:** `agents`, `memory`, `safety`, `eval`

### TL;DR

A benchmark for *memory governance* in agents whose memory is shared across multiple principals (users, organizations, roles). Measures three properties simultaneously: utility on long-horizon tasks with state updates, access control across authorization contexts, and active forgetting after explicit delete requests. Domains: medical, office, education, household.

### Key idea

Frame agent memory as an institutional governance problem, not just a retrieval problem. Each principal has its own access scope, write/read permissions, and the right to compel deletion. The benchmark forces an agent to (a) actually use shared state to do useful work, (b) refuse access when the principal isn't authorized, and (c) when something is deleted, never let it influence outputs again. Current methods optimize at most one axis.

### Main finding

No existing memory method simultaneously achieves strong utility, robust access control, and reliable forgetting — most pick two of three. The failure modes are concrete (e.g. an agent that "forgets" a fact still retrieves it via paraphrase or cross-principal leakage). The benchmark exposes these tradeoffs cleanly.

### Why it matters

Shared agent deployments (one assistant serving a department, a family, a hospital ward) are blocked on exactly this triangle. GateMem is the first public eval that puts utility, access, and forgetting in one number, so we can stop benchmarking "memory" in isolation from the deployment constraints that actually gate it.

## 5. WorldLines

**Authors:** Jianchong Su, Haojian Huang, Yifan Chang, Tianhao Zhou, Xinli Xu, Yingjie Xu, Yinchuan Li, Zexi Li, Ying-Cong Chen — *HKUST(GZ) / HKUST*
**Links:** [arXiv:2606.18847](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.18847) · [HF](https://huggingface.co/papers/2606.18847) · [AlphaXiv](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2606.18847)
**Topics:** `agents`, `multimodal`, `eval`

### TL;DR

A long-horizon embodied-agent benchmark that builds temporally extended household traces (dialogues, actions, execution feedback, object/device state changes) and turns them into evidence-linked Memory-QA and Embodied-Task-Planning samples. Also proposes ObsMem, a memory framework that keeps *visibility-aware* memories and action-native state trails so the agent's belief tracks what it has actually observed.

### Key idea

Embodied long-term memory benchmarks have been either language-only (retrieval QA) or short-horizon (single-task execution). WorldLines unifies them by attaching each evidence span to a concrete state change in a household trace and asking the agent to answer Memory-QA *and* re-plan downstream actions consistently. ObsMem's twist: memory entries carry visibility metadata, so the agent never asserts state it couldn't have observed.

### Main finding

Persistent failure modes show up cleanly: partial observability (agents hallucinate state they never saw), overwritten state (agents recall stale facts), and the language→action gap (memory recall is fine but the planner doesn't use it). ObsMem improves on this triangle by grounding memory in observation traces; it's positioned as a reference architecture rather than a SOTA point.

### Why it matters

Most "memory agent" papers conflate language memory and world-state memory. WorldLines forces them apart and shows you need both — and that visibility-awareness (knowing what you've seen vs. what you've been told) is a first-class memory primitive for embodied settings.

## 6. GeneralVLA-2

**Authors:** Haoyu Wang, Guoqing Ma, Zeyu Zhang, Yandong Guo, Boxin Shi, Hao Tang — *Peking University / CASIA / AI 2 Robotics*
**Links:** [arXiv:2606.17480](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.17480) · [HF](https://huggingface.co/papers/2606.17480) · [AlphaXiv](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2606.17480)
**Topics:** `multimodal`, `agents`, `VLA`

*Borderline: included because the "governed memory" half is method-grade and transfers to non-robotic agent settings (the paper evaluates it on Terminal-Bench 2.0 and SWE-Bench Verified, not just GSO-30).*

### TL;DR

A generalist vision-language-action system layered on a hierarchical 3D-aware interface. Two distinct contributions: (1) GeoFuse-MV3D, a geometry-prior-guided multi-view SAM3D variant that stops monocular reconstruction from hallucinating geometry; (2) "KnowledgeBank → governed long-term memory" — adds quality / confidence / lifecycle / verifier / conflict metadata to a retrieval-augmented agent memory and uses it to drive precision-oriented retrieval.

### Key idea

The agent memory side is the broadly interesting bit: instead of "embed snippet, append, retrieve by cosine," the upgraded memory tags each entry with confidence, verifier outcome, conflict signals against existing entries, and a lifecycle (active / stale / superseded). Retrieval is precision-oriented — return fewer, higher-confidence items rather than top-K cosine. This is benchmarked on agent tasks (Terminal-Bench, SWE-Bench), not just on robot pick-and-place.

### Main finding

KnowledgeBank-governed memory improves over ReasoningBank by **+4.53% on Terminal-Bench SR** and **+3.73% on SWE-Bench Verified resolve rate**, while reducing "agent steps" (AS) by 4.95% and 5.65% respectively. On the geometry side, GeoFuse-MV3D improves CD/LPIPS by 2.20%/2.02% and PSNR/SSIM by 2.36%/1.03% over MV-SAM3D.

### Why it matters

The memory-governance argument from GateMem (paper #4) gets a constructive partner here: GeneralVLA-2 shows that adding *minimal* governance metadata (confidence, lifecycle, conflicts) to an agent's retrieval-augmented memory yields measurable wins on hard coding benchmarks. Together they sketch where agent-memory research is heading.

## 7. MCompassRAG

**Authors:** Amirhossein Abaskohi, Raymond Li, Gaetano Cimino, Peter West, Giuseppe Carenini, Issam H. Laradji — *University of British Columbia / University of Salerno / ServiceNow Research*
**Links:** [arXiv:2606.18508](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.18508) · [HF](https://huggingface.co/papers/2606.18508) · [AlphaXiv](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2606.18508)
**Topics:** `RAG`, `retrieval`

### TL;DR

A metadata-guided RAG framework that breaks the granularity tradeoff (fine chunks = precise but huge search space; coarse chunks = small space but noisy embeddings) by adding a *topic-level metadata index* on top of paragraph-level chunks. Retrieval first navigates to the right topic, then runs dense similarity on a much smaller subset of paragraphs.

### Key idea

Treat topics as a navigational layer, not a representation. The dense embedding still lives on the small paragraph chunks (so precision is preserved), but the search space at query time is pruned to paragraphs whose topic metadata matches the query's topic intent. The "compass" is the topic-to-chunk index; matching is what selects the candidate pool that dense retrieval actually scores.

### Main finding

On deep-research tasks across large heterogeneous corpora, MCompassRAG beats baselines on both retrieval precision and latency — the topic prune cuts the candidate set by a large factor without losing the right paragraphs. (Exact numbers in the paper; the framing is "semantic compass" + paragraph-level retrieval, evaluated head-to-head with flat dense retrieval and LLM-rewriting baselines.)

### Why it matters

This is the unsexy but important kind of RAG paper: it's not a new embedding model or a new reranker, it's a structural argument that *metadata is cheaper than scaling embeddings* for navigating a large corpus. Plays well with deep-research agent stacks where retrieval is the bottleneck.

## 8. SproutRAG

**Authors:** Amirhossein Abaskohi, Issam H. Laradji, Peter West, Giuseppe Carenini — *University of British Columbia / ServiceNow Research*
**Links:** [arXiv:2606.18381](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.18381) · [HF](https://huggingface.co/papers/2606.18381) · [AlphaXiv](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2606.18381)
**Topics:** `RAG`, `retrieval`, `attention`

### TL;DR

A hierarchical RAG framework built on a *learned binary chunking tree*: sentence-level chunks are progressively merged into larger semantically coherent units using inter-sentence attention, and retrieval is a hierarchical beam search over the tree. Trained end-to-end with a joint objective over embeddings and tree structure — no external LLM calls, no lossy summarization.

### Key idea

Use attention itself as the chunker. The model learns which attention heads / layers best capture semantic document structure, then runs a clustering procedure over those attention maps to build a binary tree over sentences. Multi-granularity retrieval is then a hierarchical beam search that can return a sentence, a sub-tree, or a whole branch depending on the query's scope — no need to pre-commit to one chunk size.

### Main finding

**+6.1% average information-efficiency** over the strongest baseline across four benchmarks (scientific, legal, open-domain). No additional LLM calls at indexing or retrieval time — the cost story is much better than LLM-guided chunking or summarization approaches.

### Why it matters

Same family as MCompassRAG (#7), different angle: instead of *external* metadata, SproutRAG argues the chunk structure can be *learned* from the attention the retrieval model is already computing. If both styles hold up, RAG starts to look less like "fixed-chunk + dense" and more like "structured retrieval over a learned document representation."

## 9. Narrative Content in Pretraining Data

**Authors:** Teagan Johnson, Elliott Ash, Andrew Piper, Maria Antoniak — *Culture, Language, and Systems Lab*
**Links:** [arXiv:2606.19468](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.19468) · [HF](https://huggingface.co/papers/2606.19468) · [AlphaXiv](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2606.19468)
**Topics:** `data`, `analysis`

### TL;DR

A large-scale narrative-content audit of Dolma (a 3-trillion-token open pretraining corpus). The authors train **NarraBERT** (a RoBERTa-based narrative-feature classifier across 11 dimensions covering agency, setting, and events) and release **NarraDolma**, a 3M-passage labeled dataset. Result: narrative structure is measurable at scale and is very unevenly distributed across pretraining sources and topics.

### Key idea

Narrative isn't binary (story vs. non-story) — it's multidimensional. The 11-dimension framework (agency, setting, events) gives a *continuous* characterization of how narrative-shaped a passage is. NarraBERT does the per-passage scoring; NarraDolma is the resulting labeled subset that downstream researchers can use to filter, weight, or analyze pretraining mixes.

### Main finding

Narrative content is highly source-dependent (some Dolma sources are >80% narrative-rich; others <10%) and topic-dependent. The distribution is *continuous* — not a small island of "stories" inside a sea of non-narrative text. This means any mixture-design decision is implicitly making a narrative-vs-non-narrative tradeoff whether the curator knows it or not.

### Why it matters

Most data analyses of LLM pretraining corpora focus on quality and domain. This adds a structural axis — narrative-ness — that plausibly correlates with downstream behaviors (storytelling fluency, theory-of-mind, character modeling, plot coherence). It's a measurement tool more than a method, but a useful one.

## 10. Distilling Examples into Task Instructions

**Authors:** Guy Rotman, Adi Kopilov, Danit Berger Zalmanson, Omri Allouche — *(industry, primary affiliation not listed on HF page)*
**Links:** [arXiv:2606.15641](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.15641) · [HF](https://huggingface.co/papers/2606.15641) · [AlphaXiv](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2606.15641)
**Topics:** `ICL`, `prompting`, `fine-tuning`

### TL;DR

Replace few-shot in-context-learning examples with a *distilled task instruction* derived from those same examples: a compact, structured description of the classification criteria. Token count drops by ~99% and accuracy goes up; the resulting instruction is also human-editable so domain experts can refine the classifier without retraining.

### Key idea

ICL with many examples concatenated breaks down as context length grows: noise accumulates, the model gets distracted, and inference costs balloon. Instead, run a one-shot offline step where an LLM (or a structured prompt) *distills* the demonstration set into a structured description of classification criteria + an explicit task definition. At inference time, only the distilled instruction (no examples) is sent.

### Main finding

On the new **Call Playbook** dataset (5 classification tasks from real B2B sales conversations): **−99% tokens** vs. traditional ICL, **+7% macro AUC** improvement, and robustness preserved as context grows (token-compression baselines drop >9 F1 points under the same regime). Bonus: the distilled instruction is directly inspectable and editable by a human domain expert.

### Why it matters

This is "prompt engineering" reframed as a learned-but-portable artifact. The distilled instruction acts like a tiny model in its own right — you can version it, audit it, edit it — without the operational overhead of fine-tuning. For long-context-sensitive domains (legal, sales, support), this is a credible alternative to either ICL-at-scale or full fine-tuning.

## 11. StylisticBias

**Authors:** Shaghayegh Kolli, Timo Cavelius, Nafiseh Nikeghbal, Samantha Dalal, Jana Diesner — *TUM (Munich Center for ML) / Princeton CITP*
**Links:** [arXiv:2606.20527](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.20527) · [HF](https://huggingface.co/papers/2606.20527) · [AlphaXiv](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2606.20527)
**Topics:** `multimodal`, `safety`, `eval`

### TL;DR

A controlled MLLM bias benchmark that holds identity fixed and varies *one visual attribute at a time*. 500 photorealistic base faces × ~50 single-attribute variations × 6 MLLMs × 25 binary social-judgment scenarios. Finding: ~15 attributes (fashion style, body type, age) drive ~80% of social-bias variation in current MLLMs — the bias surface is concentrated, not diffuse.

### Key idea

Most MLLM bias benchmarks compare different *people*, which conflates appearance with identity. StylisticBias controls for identity by generating attribute-isolated variants of the same base face, so the measured judgment shift is attributable to the attribute swap. This lets the authors compute per-attribute sensitivities, rank them, and identify which cues actually drive model judgments.

### Main finding

Age and body type dominate identity-level effects; fashion style and other appearance cues drive the largest *attribute-level* shifts. About 15 attributes account for ~80% of the total bias variation across the 6 models tested. Sensitivity is strongest for judgments that are semantically aligned with appearance (socioeconomic, style-related), which is a structural failure mode rather than random noise.

### Why it matters

This is a usable diagnostic: instead of "this MLLM is biased" you get "this MLLM is 4× more sensitive to body type than the next one is" — a concrete, attribute-level fingerprint of an MLLM's social-judgment behavior. The "concentrated bias surface" finding also suggests that targeted attribute-level interventions could move bias metrics more than broad-spectrum SFT.
