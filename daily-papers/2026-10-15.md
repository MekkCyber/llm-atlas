## 1. ATAS

**Authors:** Weijie Wang, Haomin Zhang, Ye Li, Akide Liu, Zeyu Zhang, Haoyu Zhao, Yefei He, Donny Y. Chen, Feng Chen, Bohan Zhuang — *Zhejiang University / Monash University*
**Links:** [arXiv:2610.20571](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.20571) · [HF](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.20571) · [AlphaXiv](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2610.20571)
**Topics:** `agents`, `diffusion`, `video`, `world-model`

### TL;DR

Agentic video generation framework that treats a persistent **world state** as the shared memory for a planner-generator loop. The planner decomposes a long-horizon prompt into camera moves and scene edits; a diffusion-based generator renders each shot conditioned on the current world state; the state is updated after each shot so later shots stay geometrically and semantically consistent.

### Key idea

Separate what's being generated (pixels) from what persists (a structured world state tracked in both time — "when" — and space — "where"). The planner reads/writes this state as a scratchpad across generations, so the diffusion generator conditions on a dense, agent-authored context rather than the user prompt alone. The design pattern is a video-generation analogue of agent memory for LLM tool-use loops.

### Main finding

Reported qualitative and quantitative wins on long-horizon video generation benchmarks for consistency, scene control, and prompt fidelity. Full numbers in paper; abstract was not fully extractable from this run.

### Why it matters

Standard text-to-video models drift as horizons grow — object identities and camera geometry decay. Casting video generation as an agentic loop over a persistent world state is a plausible route to minutes-long coherent video, and it exports the "state as scratchpad" idea from LLM agents to generative media.

## 2. Player-1

**Authors:** Mingyu Ouyang, Yiran Wang, Siyuan Hu, Xingyilang Yin, Huiqiang Sun, Guangzhi Wang, Junfu Pu, Cheng Cheng, Honghao Chen, Ying Shan, Mike Zheng Shou, Hwee Tou Ng — *NUS / ARC Lab Tencent IEG / NTU*
**Links:** [arXiv:2610.20584](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.20584) · [HF](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.20584) · [AlphaXiv](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2610.20584)
**Topics:** `agents`, `RL`, `synthetic-data`, `computer-use`

### TL;DR

Game-playing computer-use agent trained in two stages: first SFT on curated gameplay recordings, then RL in 16 synthetic 2D/3D environments whose automatically-checkable rewards act as a verifier. The resulting policy improves on Minecraft tasks and transfers to unseen games.

### Key idea

Treat gameplay environments as a cheap, large-scale **synthetic experience** source for computer-use post-training. The 16 environments span varied genres and are instrumented to emit programmatic reward signals, so GRPO-style RL has clean, verifiable rewards — the game is the verifier.

### Main finding

Measured improvements on Minecraft task success and positive transfer to out-of-distribution games (full numbers in paper). The paper highlights that synthetic game environments provide the kind of verifiable reward surface RLVR needs for agent training.

### Why it matters

Agent RL stalls on reward design — real tasks lack automatic checkers. Games are a near-infinite supply of auto-checkable action trajectories; if transfer is real, this is a scalable alternative to hand-curated agent benchmarks. Related in spirit to robotics simulators that train VLAs offline.

## 3. Road MOPD

**Authors:** Shi-Yu Tian, Ximan Liu, Tianrui Zhu, Sule Bai, Xinwei Yang, Ruiqi Liu, Kun-Yang Yu, Ming Yang, Zhi Zhou, Yu-Feng Li, Yipeng Sun, Gu Simiu — *Nanjing University / JD.com*
**Links:** [arXiv:2610.13521](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.13521) · [HF](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.13521) · [AlphaXiv](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2610.13521)
**Topics:** `distillation`, `post-training`

### TL;DR

Multi-teacher on-policy distillation (OPD) usually routes each domain to its own expert teacher, but the student still shares weights across domains and the gradients fight each other. Road MOPD adds the *non-routed* teachers as cross-domain critics on the same student trajectories, uses agreement-guided token-level credit assignment, and applies preventive gradient projection with an adaptive norm-retention floor.

### Key idea

Teachers that *aren't* responsible for the current domain are reused as independent "guards": they score the student's rollouts for the current domain, and their agreement with the routed teacher is turned into token-level advantages. A gradient-projection step then removes components that would damage the disadvantaged teacher's domain, with an adaptive floor that keeps the step from vanishing.

### Main finding

Reduces degradation in the disadvantaged domain, gains >5 points under high-conflict settings, improves overall in low-conflict and three-domain settings. Baseline is standard domain-routed multi-teacher OPD.

### Why it matters

Multi-domain post-training is the dominant setting for production RL (math + code + chat + safety). Standard multi-teacher OPD silently loses capability on the "losing" domain whenever teachers disagree. Road MOPD is a drop-in that treats cross-domain conflict as a first-class signal — a reusable move for any multi-task RL pipeline.

## 4. Self-Distillation Reality Check

**Authors:** Linhao Zhong, Bozhen Fang, Xuehao Wang, Duochao Shi, Zekai Luo, Guangming Yao, Yi Yuan, Hao Chen, Jingdong Chen, Chunhua Shen — *Zhejiang University*
**Links:** [arXiv:2610.19789](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.19789) · [HF](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.19789) · [AlphaXiv](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2610.19789)
**Topics:** `agents`, `RL`, `distillation`, `eval`

### TL;DR

Benchmarks 10 self-distillation methods against vanilla GRPO on ALFWorld, ScienceWorld, Search-QA, and WebShop across three model sizes — 132 training runs in total — and finds that none consistently beats GRPO. Final-score gains are small, training is often unstable, and extra compute does not reliably help.

### Key idea

Rather than propose a new method, run a controlled bake-off: ten published self-distillation and self-rewarding variants, same backbones (small/medium/large), same environments, same compute envelope. Report mean final score, within-run stability, and cost — not just a cherry-picked best run.

### Main finding

**(a)** Gains over GRPO are typically within noise; no method is a consistent winner across tasks/sizes. **(b)** Strong final scores often hide training-time instability (sharp drops, divergence). **(c)** Doubling or tripling compute via self-distillation adds variance, not accuracy.

### Why it matters

Important corrective to the self-distillation / self-rewarding-RL literature: results on one benchmark + one backbone don't transfer. For teams choosing between vanilla GRPO and a flashy "self-X" variant, this is empirical evidence to stay with the baseline unless the new method is well-matched to the task.

## 5. CriticOPD

**Authors:** Tianrun Yu, Shangzhe Li, Yuxiao Yang, Amanda Hughes, Weitong Zhang, Taylor W. Killian — *BYU / UNC Chapel Hill*
**Links:** [arXiv:2610.20572](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.20572) · [HF](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.20572) · [AlphaXiv](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2610.20572)
**Topics:** `distillation`, `post-training`, `reasoning`

### TL;DR

Critique-guided on-policy distillation. For an incorrect student rollout the teacher writes a critique, the student rewrites from the last error, and the teacher then re-scores the revision *with the critique in context* while the student trains on the revision *without* the critique.

### Key idea

Keep the student on-policy (training on its own rollouts) but give the teacher a *locus* to supervise — the specific point in the trace where the student went wrong. The critique lets the teacher produce dense token-level supervision over a plausibly reachable revision, instead of pointing at a far-away gold answer. The asymmetric context (teacher sees critique, student doesn't) means the student has to internalize the correction, not copy it.

### Main finding

Across two Qwen3 student sizes and six math-reasoning benchmarks, CriticOPD beats standard OPD, with the biggest gains on competition problems.

### Why it matters

Standard OPD scales poorly when the student rollout is mostly wrong — the teacher's token-level signal collapses to "do everything differently." Critique-guided OPD breaks the trace into a prefix the student already handles and a suffix the teacher can usefully correct. Should transfer to code and tool-use distillation where mid-trace mistakes dominate.

## 6. LatentLoop

**Authors:** Yunkai Chai, Xiaoyang Chen, Tong Zhu, Zongliang Wu, Yun Luo, Yafu Li, Yu Cheng — *SJTU / Shanghai AI Lab / USTC / CUHK / NTU*
**Links:** [arXiv:2610.19975](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.19975) · [HF](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.19975) · [AlphaXiv](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2610.19975)
**Topics:** `MoE`, `architectures`, `efficient`

### TL;DR

Looped MoE models (where the same expert stack is called more than once) have been treated symmetrically — same compute per visit. LatentLoop observes the two visits prefer different residual scales, compresses the sequence on the first visit, keeps full resolution on the second, redirects the saved compute into model *width*, and learns per-layer writeback coefficients.

### Key idea

Asymmetric compute allocation across loop iterations: the first pass runs on a shorter latent sequence (lower effective tokens), the second pass runs at full length to decode. The budget saved from compressing the first pass funds more experts/width, and learned writeback coefficients mix the two visits' residuals instead of adding them 1:1.

### Main finding

Across 15 configurations from 1.3B to 7B parameters, LatentLoop lowers validation loss and improves nine-benchmark average; the paper claims predicted compute savings of 19.0% vs vanilla MoE and 7.9% vs the looped baseline.

### Why it matters

MoE scaling has stalled on expert-utilization and all-to-all costs; parameter-sharing by looping is a complementary knob. LatentLoop is the first paper to show that **loop iterations are not symmetric** — a design observation that applies to any parameter-sharing architecture (Universal Transformer, Albert-like weight tying, DeepSeek-MoE loops).

## 7. Cortex

**Authors:** Jintian Zhang, Feng He, Xinnian Liang, Ziwen Xu, Haoming Xu, Haojing Huang, Shuangzhi Wu, Ningyu Zhang — *ByteDance Seed*
**Links:** [arXiv:2610.20399](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.20399) · [HF](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.20399) · [AlphaXiv](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2610.20399)
**Topics:** `memory`, `LoRA`, `interp`, `editing`

### TL;DR

Context improves LLM performance, but the gains vanish when the context is removed. Cortex consolidates those gains into **mergeable low-rank or structural FFN updates** by first generating prospective queries that anticipate how the context will be used, then distilling context-conditioned outputs into weight-space edits.

### Key idea

Memory Consolidation (MC): make the model behave as if the context were still present, without it in the prompt — but keep every non-target capability intact. Cortex implements this with two steps: (1) *prospective query generation* — the model samples future-use queries from the context so the distillation set covers what the context would have been asked about; (2) distill the context-conditioned outputs on these queries into low-rank or structural FFN deltas that merge cleanly into the base.

### Main finding

Across 4B–120B dense and MoE backbones, text-only and multimodal, Cortex lies on the empirical consolidation–retention Pareto frontier in most evaluated settings. After 50 sequential factual edits on a 14B model, Cortex-Structural recovers **88.9%** of the context-induced gain while retaining **99.8%** of non-target performance.

### Why it matters

Puts context-based prompting, knowledge editing, skill learning, and activation steering under one objective (internalize gains, preserve the rest). The 99.8% retention after 50 sequential edits is the most interesting number — sequential edit methods usually degrade non-target performance significantly, so a near-flat retention curve is a step change for continual model updating.

## 8. ActionOPD

**Authors:** (not fully extractable from HF page)
**Links:** [arXiv:2610.19820](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.19820) · [HF](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.19820) · [AlphaXiv](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2610.19820)
**Topics:** `agents`, `distillation`, `RL`

### TL;DR

Action-level on-policy distillation that does not require token probabilities or a shared tokenizer with the teacher. The student is rewarded for taking actions semantically equivalent to actions the teacher would take at the states the student visits.

### Key idea

Shift distillation from the token level to the action/decision level. The teacher is used as a black-box policy (API-only is enough): sample actions from the teacher at student-visited states, grade the student's chosen action for semantic agreement, and use that as RL reward. Also introduces Self-ActionOPD, which uses the same model with privileged context as a teacher of itself.

### Main finding

On Qwen3-4B and Qwen3-8B, average gains of **3.3–6.9 points** across four out-of-distribution tool-use benchmarks over strong distillation baselines.

### Why it matters

Removes two deep practical constraints on distillation: shared tokenizers and access to teacher logits. For API-only teachers (frontier closed models) and heterogeneous student architectures, action-level agreement is the right unit of supervision. Important for the long tail of production distillation where only prompt/response access to the teacher is available.

## 9. ReMaRk

**Authors:** Hao Jiang, Jinghao Guo, Weisi Lin, Chau Yuen — *Nanyang Technological University*
**Links:** [arXiv:2610.17771](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.17771) · [HF](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.17771) · [AlphaXiv](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2610.17771)
**Topics:** `RL`, `GRPO`, `recommenders`

### TL;DR

When many humans reply to the same recommendation prompt with different valid answers, standard GRPO treats each one as the single ground truth per training example — "responder noise." ReMaRk scores the generated list against the **union** of all human replies, cutting RL compute while matching recall.

### Key idea

Treat the training example as a *set* of valid answers (all human replies to the same prompt) rather than one. Compute RL reward against this set, not a single reply. The same technique works on ProtoQA (commonsense Q&A with multiple accepted answers per question).

### Main finding

Comparable validation recall with substantially less RL compute across seven backbones and three Reddit-based recommendation datasets; also improves ProtoQA.

### Why it matters

Multi-right-answer domains (recommendation, open-ended QA, suggestion, creative writing) are the native habitat for a lot of production RL. Modeling the reward as set-vs-list, not list-vs-single, is a cheap fix that avoids wasting compute training against noise.

## 10. KV4-RL

**Authors:** Sihwan Park, Sung-Yub Kim, Doohyuk Jang, Eunho Yang (and others) — *NAVER Cloud / KAIST / AITRICS*
**Links:** [arXiv:2610.14448](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.14448) · [HF](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.14448) · [AlphaXiv](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2610.14448)
**Topics:** `RL`, `quantization`, `efficient-inference`, `rollouts`

### TL;DR

Rollouts dominate RL post-training wall-clock. KV4-RL quantizes the rollout KV cache to **INT4** while keeping attention-sink and recent-context positions at INT8 to control sampling-mismatch drift. On Qwen3-8B-Base GRPO, it delivers **1.80× rollout speedup** and **1.27× full-iteration speedup** vs BF16, with final six-benchmark mean within 0.5 points of BF16.

### Key idea

The KV cache is read many times during rollout decoding; quantizing it attacks a bandwidth-bound bottleneck without touching training weights. The trick is that aggressive quantization distorts the rollout *distribution*, so KV4-RL keeps high-leverage positions (sinks, recent tokens) at higher precision, limiting the policy-sample mismatch that would otherwise bias the RL gradient.

### Main finding

Across 400 iterations of Qwen3-8B-Base GRPO: 1.80× cumulative rollout speedup, 1.27× full-iteration speedup, accuracy within 0.5pp of BF16 for both 4B and 8B models.

### Why it matters

RL rollouts are the single most expensive phase of modern post-training. Quantizing *rollout-only* caches (not training) is a cheap, surgical speedup that most reasoning-RL stacks (vLLM-driven rollout, veRL, OpenRLHF) can adopt. INT4-with-INT8-sinks is a reusable recipe.

## 11. Long-Horizon RL Needs Muon

**Authors:** Kai Ruan, Jinghao Lin, Tao Chen, Ziqi Zhou, Qianshan Wei, Zihe Huang — *Duke / Institute of Automation CAS / Institute of Computing Technology CAS*
**Links:** [arXiv:2610.20256](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.20256) · [HF](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.20256) · [AlphaXiv](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2610.20256)
**Topics:** `RL`, `agents`, `optimizer`, `long-horizon`

### TL;DR

For single-rollout **asynchronous** RL on long-horizon agent tasks (hundreds of steps, dozens of tool calls), the authors argue AdamW is a bottleneck. They swap in the **Muon** optimizer and combine it with token-averaged loss, truncated importance sampling, and auxiliary length/tool-error penalties. On SWE-style repository-level issue resolution with two model families, the recipe trains more successfully than the baselines.

### Key idea

Long-horizon agent trajectories have *very long credit assignment* and highly skewed gradient directions (a few steps carry most of the signal). Muon's matrix-orthogonalized updates are empirically better conditioned for this regime than AdamW. Combined with token-averaged loss (so long rollouts don't dominate), truncated importance sampling (so off-policy drift doesn't explode), and task-shaped penalties, the full stack trains on 100+-step tool-use trajectories without collapsing.

### Main finding

Higher training success and better final scores than AdamW-based baselines on repository-level issue resolution across two model families. (Exact numbers in paper; abstract only.)

### Why it matters

Agent RL at long horizons has mostly meant very short rollouts. This paper is one of the first to publicly argue that the choice of **optimizer** matters at the step-count where tool-use agents actually live. Muon has been quietly adopted in recent frontier training; this is explicit guidance for post-training stacks.

## 12. Apertus 1.5

**Authors:** Yixuan Xu, Yassine Turki, Vinko Sabolčec, Valentina Pyatkin, Antoine Bosselut, Martin Jaggi, Imanol Schlag, + ~100 collaborators — *ETH Zurich / EPFL / HES-SO Valais-Wallis / FHNW / SDSC / CSCS / Univ. Zurich / Univ. Bern*
**Links:** [arXiv:2610.15542](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.15542) · [HF](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.15542) · [AlphaXiv](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2610.15542)
**Topics:** `multimodal`, `tech-report`, `open-weights`, `multilingual`

### TL;DR

Full Swiss-stack open release: Apertus 1.5 extends Apertus 1.0 by (1) continued pretraining on additional text, image, and audio tokens, (2) a 4× context extension to **262,144 tokens**, and (3) a multi-stage post-training that turns a text model into a multimodal reasoner. Weights and recipes are released under permissive licenses.

### Key idea

Open-infrastructure LM: every component (data, training code, intermediate checkpoints, eval harness) is released. The paper is organized as a full tech report — architecture, continued pretraining mix, context extension recipe, multimodal adapter training, post-training stages, red-teaming, evaluation.

### Main finding

## 13. CaLIF

**Authors:** Wooseok Seo, Hamish Ivison, Hyesoo Hong, Donghoon Kim, Wonjun Kang, Albert No, Jaehyung Kim — *Yonsei / UW / FuriosaAI*
**Links:** [arXiv:2610.19525](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.19525) · [HF](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.19525) · [AlphaXiv](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2610.19525)
**Topics:** `RL`, `safety`, `eval`, `instruction-following`

### TL;DR

Verifiable instruction-following benchmarks (IFEval, IFBench, ...) grade whether constraints are met, not whether the response actually answers the underlying request. Training only on the verifier's score produces reward hacking — constraint satisfaction up, response quality down. CaLIF adds a bonus from a **calibrated likelihood region** of the frozen reference policy, pulling the model back toward answers the base model would plausibly produce.

### Key idea

The verifier is a necessary but not sufficient signal. Shape the RL reward with a term that bounds the policy inside a calibrated likelihood region around the reference — not a hard KL budget, not a learned RM, but a frozen-policy-defined admissible set that constrains how far "answer quality" can degrade while chasing the verifier.

### Main finding

Better IFBench constraint satisfaction with maintained response quality, plus gains on three rubric-based benchmarks. The paper frames this as a diagnosis + correction: it both identifies the specific hacking failure mode (quality collapse) and provides a fix.

### Why it matters

The field is standardizing on IFEval-style verifiable rewards, which quietly admit the same reward-hacking pathology that killed PRM-based reasoning RL. CaLIF is a diagnostic contribution first (name the failure mode) and a fix second — most teams running IF-RL stacks will want to run their own hacking audit.

## 14. JSSA

**Authors:** Jiwon Song, Yoongon Kim, Jae-Joon Kim — *Seoul National University VLSI Lab*
**Links:** [arXiv:2610.19026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.19026) · [HF](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.19026) · [AlphaXiv](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2610.19026)
**Topics:** `efficient-inference`, `attention`, `long-context`

### TL;DR

Long-context decoding is dominated by KV-cache bandwidth. Joint Subspace Sparse Attention learns a shared low-dimensional query-key subspace to pick which tokens to attend to and uses a gather-free kernel so the sparse set can be scored without a scatter/gather over the KV cache.

### Key idea

Previous sparse-attention methods pay for the sparsity with a scatter/gather over the KV cache that eats most of the theoretical savings. JSSA projects Q and K into a shared learned low-rank subspace (one subspace, not one per head), computes approximate scores there, and the kernel reads the selected positions in contiguous order — no gather.

### Main finding

Reduces KV-cache traffic on long-context decoding; the paper reports latency improvements under long prefill + long generation (exact numbers in paper).

### Why it matters

Long-context attention is the active frontier for inference systems (vLLM, SGLang, TensorRT-LLM). The *gather* step has quietly been the real bottleneck for sparse-attention methods; a gather-free kernel changes the arithmetic of what's worth implementing. If reproducible, JSSA replaces current top-k attention variants.

## 15. Thinking Leakage

**Authors:** Shuming Liu, Bang An, Aznaur Aliev, Bing Li, Yibo Yang, Bernard Ghanem — *KAUST / Shanghai Jiao Tong University*
**Links:** [arXiv:2610.14270](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.14270) · [HF](https://huggingface.co/papers/2610.14270) · [AlphaXiv](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2610.14270)
**Topics:** `RL`, `reasoning`, `interp`

### TL;DR

When GRPO trains a hybrid-reasoning model (Qwen3 1.7B/4B/8B) in **no-think mode** — explicitly told not to show CoT — most of the accuracy gain comes from the model silently reactivating its think-mode reasoning. Weight-scaling, activation-subspace patching, and attention-head experiments all pin the effect to the GRPO advantage signal.

### Key idea

Causally probe the no-think policy with three interventions: scale the think-mode weight delta up/down, patch activation subspaces between think and no-think modes, and ablate heads known to be think-mode-selective. The gain under GRPO moves with these interventions, strongly implying the policy is *leaking* reasoning behavior under the no-think prompt.

### Main finding

Across three Qwen3 sizes, GRPO on hybrid-reasoning models in no-think mode improves accuracy mostly by activating the think-mode circuit, not by learning a new no-think strategy. The paper links this directly to the sign/scale of the GRPO advantage.

### Why it matters

"No-think" RL has been pitched as a way to make models reason implicitly. This paper is the first clear evidence that it isn't learning implicit reasoning — it's quietly turning CoT back on inside the forward pass. Significant for anyone trying to train latency-constrained reasoners, and a methodological warning: measuring "no CoT accuracy" without controls over mode-switching is misleading.
