# Thought-level beam search (Gambit)

*Depth — beam search over reasoning traces at *thought* granularity, pruning full-thought units instead of tokens or full rollouts.*

**TL;DR:** Test-time compute is the dominant lever for large-reasoning-model quality, but both **parallel sampling** (uniform compute across independent rollouts) and **MCTS** (per-node expansion overhead) misallocate it. **Gambit** (Yang et al., 2026) does beam search at the granularity of a *thought*: at each thought boundary it keeps the top-$K$ most promising ongoing reasoning traces, prunes the rest, and continues. Result on two hard math benchmarks: **+6.7 pts on HMMT-24, +3.3 pts on AIME-25** while **reducing token consumption by 68.5%** vs. parallel sampling.

## What it is

Reasoning models emit long chains of thought composed of discrete *thoughts* — units delimited by paragraph breaks, explicit `\n\n`, or model-emitted separators. Between thoughts the model has a natural pause where partial-solution quality can be scored. Gambit exploits this by treating reasoning as a **sequential decision problem at thought granularity**:

- **State** = the partial CoT up to the current thought boundary.
- **Action** = the next thought (generated by continuing the model until the next boundary).
- **Beam** = top-$K$ partial CoTs kept alive at each frontier.
- **Score** = a lightweight verifier / reward model on the partial trace.

At each boundary Gambit expands every live beam member by generating one new thought, scores the expanded set, and keeps the top $K$.

## How it works

Per prompt:

```
beam = [empty_trace]
while any trace not terminated:
    candidates = []
    for trace in beam:
        for _ in range(branching_factor):
            next_thought = LM.generate_one_thought(prompt, trace)
            candidates.append(trace + next_thought)
    beam = top_K(candidates, key=score)
return best(beam, key=final_score)
```

Two key components:

- **Thought detection.** The paper uses a rule-based delimiter (double newline / explicit tag) that works reliably on r1/o1-style traces. Robustness improves when the model was RL-trained to emit explicit thought boundaries.
- **Scoring.** Any partial-trace scorer works — outcome-model heuristics, PRM-style step scoring, or even the model's own log-probability on a self-critique prompt. Cheap scoring keeps the algorithm dominated by generation cost.

The knobs are $K$ (beam width) and the branching factor (# expansions per beam member per round). Larger $K$ = closer to parallel sampling; smaller $K$ = closer to greedy. Gambit's sweet spot sits between them.

## Why it matters

- **Cheaper than parallel sampling at matched accuracy.** Parallel sampling wastes compute on branches that are already visibly wrong halfway through. Thought-level pruning kills those branches early.
- **Cheaper than MCTS at similar-or-better accuracy.** MCTS spends compute on expansion / rollout / backprop bookkeeping; Gambit skips all of it.
- **Drop-in for served reasoning endpoints.** No training required — Gambit is an inference-time algorithm that wraps a trained reasoning model.
- **Composable with length penalties.** The scorer can incorporate length so Gambit rewards efficient reasoning as well as correct reasoning.

## Gotchas & tricks

- **Scorer quality is everything.** A miscalibrated partial-trace scorer prunes correct branches early and locks the beam into wrong solutions. Empirically simple scorers work, but audit the pruning decisions.
- **Thought boundary detection failures.** If the model doesn't emit reliable boundaries, the "thoughts" get uneven and beam scoring becomes noisy. RL-tune the model to emit boundary tokens where possible.
- **Beam collapse.** If the top-$K$ traces are near-duplicates (which happens when the model is over-confident), diversity in the beam evaporates. Add a diversity bonus or force-branch on divergent tokens.
- **Not for tasks without partial-quality signal.** If the answer is a single boolean at the end and there's no useful intermediate score, Gambit degenerates to parallel sampling.
