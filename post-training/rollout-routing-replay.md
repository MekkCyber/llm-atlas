# Rollout Routing Replay (R3)

*Depth — record each MoE layer's per-token expert-routing decisions during rollout, then replay them during the gradient update so actor and sampler see the same experts.*

**TL;DR:** RL post-training an MoE agent is unstable because the router is *stochastic*: the same token in rollout may hit a different expert during the backward pass, producing an actor/sampler mismatch that compounds turn by turn. **Rollout Routing Replay (R3)** records the sampler's per-token expert choices at every MoE layer during rollout, then *replays* them during the training forward pass — the gradient is computed against the exact routing the sampler used. On T1 (122B MoE, terminal agent, 300+ turns), R3 cuts the train-vs-inference log-probability gap from 0.021 to 0.013 and enables stable long-horizon RL.

## What it is

An MoE layer routes each token to a small subset (usually top-1 or top-2) of experts. Routing is decided by a router network whose top-k selection is *point-wise deterministic* given the router logits — but router logits themselves depend on the token's activations, which shift slightly between rollout inference and the training forward pass (numerics, kernel differences, dropout, or subtle batch differences). Small shifts flip top-k boundaries; different experts fire.

The result: the actor policy the rollout *sampled from* is not the policy the *gradient is computed against*. On short trajectories the effect is small. On 300+-turn terminal-agent rollouts it compounds into a large train-inference log-probability gap and unstable optimization.

R3 removes the ambiguity by *recording* the sampler's routing decisions and forcing training to use them.

## How it works

**Rollout side.** For each token generated by the sampler, at every MoE layer, record the indices of the experts the sampler routed to. Persist alongside the token IDs (see also [TITO](tito.md), which records the token IDs themselves).

**Training side.** During the forward pass on the sampled trajectory, bypass the router's re-selection and inject the recorded expert indices at each MoE layer. The router's gating scores are still used for the gating-value weighting (so gradients flow through the router), but the expert selection is fixed to what the sampler chose.

Concretely, the MoE layer's forward becomes:

```
scores = router(x)                          # same as usual
gates  = softmax(scores)
experts_used = recorded_indices[t, layer]   # from rollout
y = sum(gates[e] * experts[e](x) for e in experts_used)
```

Gradients flow through both the gates and the experts; the top-k step is skipped.

## Why it matters

- **Eliminates a compounding drift source.** Actor/sampler mismatch is silent in short-CoT RL but catastrophic in long-horizon agentic RL. R3 removes it at the root.
- **Empirical evidence on T1.** Train-inference log-prob gap 0.021 → 0.013; contributes to the base-model jump from 43.8% → 64.0% on Terminal-Bench 2.1.
- **Composes with TITO.** TITO records token IDs (avoiding tokenizer/sampler retokenization drift); R3 records routing (avoiding router drift). Together they align rollout and training at the token+expert level.

## Gotchas & tricks

- **Storage cost is real.** Recording every expert index at every MoE layer for every token multiplies rollout data volume. In practice indices are small ints so overhead is manageable; still budget for it.
- **Only helps if the sampler's routing is what you want.** If the sampler is buggy (wrong precision, missing bias), R3 will faithfully preserve the bug. Validate the sampler's routing distribution before enabling.
- **Doesn't remove the router entirely.** Router gradients still flow through the gating values — the router *learns*, it just doesn't get to re-decide expert selection at training time.
- **Complementary to aux-loss-free load balancing.** R3 makes actor/sampler consistent per-token; load-balancing keeps aggregate expert utilization sane. Both are needed for stable MoE RL.
